                       A SHORT IBM PC HISTORY
                       ======================

1981. IBM ships the 5150 Personal Computer. Intel 8088 at 4.77 MHz,
16 to 256 KB of RAM, one or two single-sided 5.25-inch floppies, no
hard disk. PC-DOS 1.0 is on a floppy you boot from. List price:
$1,565 for the floppy-less cassette model, $3,000+ once you put two
drives and a monitor on it. Adjusted for the dollar's loss of
weight, that is real money.

The architecture was open. IBM published the BIOS source in the
Technical Reference manual. This was either a strategic decision or
an accident; opinions differ. Either way, by 1983 Compaq had
reverse-engineered the BIOS clean-room and was shipping a portable
clone, and the floodgates were open.

1983. The IBM PC XT. 10 MB hard disk. The first machine where you
might genuinely keep more than one program installed at a time.
PC-DOS 2.0 supports subdirectories. People accustomed to a flat
disk treat this with appropriate suspicion.

1984. The IBM PC AT. Intel 80286 at 6 MHz, eventually 8. 16-bit ISA
bus. Sixteen colors if you bought the EGA card. AT-class machines
were the first that could run something resembling Unix; SCO XENIX
shipped on AT-and-up by mid-decade.

1986. Compaq ships the Deskpro 386 — an Intel 80386 machine — eight
months before IBM does. The point at which IBM stops setting the
pace and the rest of the market starts.

1987. PS/2 line. Microchannel bus, OS/2 1.0, 3.5-inch floppy, VGA
graphics. IBM tries to take the architecture back. Largely fails;
the clones keep ISA and the market keeps clones.

1991. Where you are reading this. 386 and 486 machines. 4 to 16 MB
of RAM. SVGA at 800x600 if you spent the money. Wholesale shift to
Windows 3.0 underway. UNIX boxes still exist on campuses and at
banks. BSD 4.2 still runs on a VAX in a closet somewhere — possibly
this closet.

What this matters for: software written when the 8088 was new
assumed almost nothing about its environment. There was no
protected mode. There was no separation of process address spaces.
There were no execute-only pages. The buffer-overflow bug class
predates all of those and assumes none of them. The PC came of age
in a security model that had not yet learned to be one.

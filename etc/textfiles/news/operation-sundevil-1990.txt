                    OPERATION SUNDEVIL -- A SUMMARY
                    ===============================

      Compiled from the public record. Use as a starting point;
      Bruce Sterling's "The Hacker Crackdown" (1992) is the
      definitive book on the period if you want depth.

[--[ WHAT HAPPENED ]--]

On May 8 and 9, 1990, U.S. Secret Service agents executed search
warrants in fourteen U.S. cities -- among them Phoenix, Newark,
Tucson, Cincinnati, Pittsburgh, Detroit, San Diego, Los Angeles, San
Jose, Plano, San Francisco, Richmond, Chicago, and New York. The
operation was named SunDevil after the Arizona State University
mascot, reflecting that the U.S. Attorney for Arizona was the lead.

Forty-two computer systems were seized. Over twenty thousand
floppy disks were carted off. Several BBSs went dark with no
warning to their users; some were never returned to their owners.

The stated targets were credit-card and long-distance-toll fraud
rings. The actual scope, as it became clear over the following
months, was considerably broader and considerably less precise.

[--[ THE STEVE JACKSON GAMES RAID ]--]

Not technically part of Sundevil, but executed by the same Secret
Service unit two months earlier (March 1, 1990) and conflated with
it in popular memory: agents raided the offices of Steve Jackson
Games in Austin. They seized the company's BBS, several computers,
and the manuscript drafts of an unreleased role-playing game called
GURPS Cyberpunk. The raid was based on the assertion that one of
SJG's employees had received a copy of an internal BellSouth
document concerning the 911 system -- a document that, by the time
the case went to trial in 1993, was shown to be available from
BellSouth itself for $13.

Steve Jackson Games sued the federal government and won. The case
was the foundational fact for the Electronic Frontier Foundation,
which incorporated in July 1990 specifically to address the legal
posture of computer users in the wake of these raids.

[--[ WHAT MATTERS ABOUT IT ]--]

The Sundevil raids and the Steve Jackson Games case did three
things that still shape the field today:

  1. They established that the line between "system administrator
     who knows things" and "criminal" was, at the time, drawn
     almost entirely by the prosecutor. People who ran BBSs
     learned to expect that anything on their disk could be
     evidence, and that being a hobbyist sysop did not protect
     them from a 4 AM door knock.

  2. They created the political conditions for the EFF, the
     Computer Fraud and Abuse Act case law that followed, and
     the long-running argument over whether security research
     can be conducted lawfully at all.

  3. They taught the underground that PR matters. The raids made
     "hacker" a household word for the first time, and the
     defenders -- Steve Jackson, Mitch Kapor, John Perry Barlow --
     framed the conversation in terms of civil liberties rather
     than criminal mischief. That framing largely held.
